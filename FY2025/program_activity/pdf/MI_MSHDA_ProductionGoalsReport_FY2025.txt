RE: FY 2024-2025 Housing Production Goals Report

Section 32(14) of P.A. 346 of 1966 requires the Michigan State Housing Development
Authority (MSHDA) to provide the Governor and the appropriate legislative committees with
an annual report on housing projects financed by the Authority. This document represents an
assessment of FY 2025 production and the Authority's goals for FY 2026. It also addresses
the boilerplate reporting requirements in Section 401 of Public Act 119 of 2023 and Section
403 of Public Act 22 of 2025. Please note that the Authority's 2025 fiscal year ran from July 1,
2024, through June 30, 2025.

In fiscal year 2024-2025, MSHDA's total investment in housing for low- and moderate-income
Michiganders was $2.610 billion, serving nearly 45,000 households. MSHDA contributed
$2.193 billion in funding toward home mortgages or newly constructed, rehabilitated, and
financed units of housing, resulting in 10,569 new, improved, or newly owned units; $287
million in rental assistance; $28.8 million in Homeless Prevention grants; $50.7 million in
Neighborhood Development grants, and $49.8 million in Housing Solutions grants. Across all
programs, MSHDA surpassed its goal by producing or financing the rehabilitation or
purchase of 12,414 housing units, nearly 2,000 more than the FY's goal. Goals for individual
programs and divisions are noted in the following pages of this document.

Exhibit 5. FY 2024-2025 Changes to QAP for 2026-2027

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program has long been one of the most impactful resources that Michigan has for developing affordable
rental housing. As statewide housing needs and priorities evolve, so does the Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP), which sets the guidelines and priorities
for the LIHTC program. MSHDA drafted changes to the QAP to reflect these priorities throughout FY 2024-2025, with the final 2026-2027 QAP receiving
MSHDA's Board approval in June 2025 and Governor Whitmer's approval in July 2025.

The 2026-2027 QAP includes new initiatives that align with recent significant progress that has been made in Michigan's statewide housing policy.
These new initiatives are described in further detail below and cover the following topic areas:

    1. Job Growth Opportunities: Creating affordable housing around new Job Centers
    2. Community Supported Initiatives: Alignment with local housing policy
    3. Tribal Housing: Alignment of Tribal Housing policies with Tribal Housing needs

To address these focus areas, several changes have been implemented throughout the QAP, scoring criteria, and accompanying program documents.
Following is a list of the more significant revisions to the 2026-2027 Qualified Allocation Plan, scoring criteria, and other related policies; it is not
intended to be all-encompassing. Therefore, in addition to reviewing this list, stakeholders are encouraged to review all of the program documents in
their entirety to gain a full understanding of the policies and program requirements contained within them.
JOB GROWTH OPPORTUNITIES: CREATING HOUSING AROUND NEW JOB CENTERS
According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics and Builder Permits Survey from the US Census Bureau, between 2014 and 2023, the United States
produced one (1) new housing unit for every six (6) new jobs. In Michigan that ratio was much worse - we created one (1) new housing unit for every
fourteen (14) new jobs created. This is a ratio that is unsustainable for real growth in Michigan.
In February of 2025, MSHDA launched the Employer-Assisted Housing Fund (EAHF), designed to provide employers with an easy entry-point to begin
investing in housing for the local workforce. The EAHF provides MSHDA matching funds to large and small employers that are committed to investing in
various forms of housing.

Further, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) also recently reported that in 2022 the median wage for the "Top 5" occupation groups is below $40k per
year. This means that for those new jobs, a rent of about $1,000 per month is necessary for household to NOT be "housing burdened."
Building on the goals of the EAHF, the 2026-2027 QAP includes "Job Growth Opportunities" points for projects that are located within 15 or 25 miles of
significant economic development projects funded by the Michigan Economic Development Corporation. This not only aligns new affordable housing
with new jobs, but also further aligns the work being done across state agencies.

COMMUNITY SUPPORTED INITIATIVES: ALIGNMENT WITH LOCAL HOUSING POLICIES
In June of 2022, Michigan released its first-ever Statewide Housing Plan as a bold response to the state's affordable housing crisis. Fifteen Regional
Housing Partnerships were established to assist with the implementation of the Plan, as well as develop regional action plans - each with regional
priorities. A hallmark of the Statewide Housing Plan and its implementation is recognizing that each community has individualized local needs and
alignment of state and local partners is critical to meeting those needs most productively.

Recognizing this, the 2026-2027 QAP includes "Community Supported Initiatives" points for projects that align with local initiatives and utilize place-
based developers. Developments located or developed in accordance with local unit of government approval, through a community-supported
development plan, request for proposal, and/or other specific project alignment with community programming will be eligible for this point item.
Additional points will be available for developments that include a place-based developer with at least 25% ownership in the development.
These new criteria will identify and promote developments and developers that are directly responsive to the unique and documented housing needs of
the local community.

TRIBAL HOUSING: ALIGNMENT OF TRIBAL HOUSING POLICIES WITH TRIBAL HOUSING NEEDS
In 2024, in anticipation of the upcoming QAP changes, the United Tribes of Michigan, on behalf of its members and the future of tribal housing in
Michigan, requested that MSHDA honor a request for Consultation on the QAP in which the Michigan Tribes are afforded a meaningful opportunity to
advocate for change leading to implementation of a fair and equitable process that recognizes the unique housing needs of Tribal communities as well
as the capacity of the Michigan Tribes to successfully administer LIHTC projects.
In the following months, MSHDA staff and representatives from various Michigan Tribes participated in multiple formal Consultation meetings to
discuss the QAP and its policies. MSHDA staff deeply appreciated this opportunity as it highlighted some significant deficiencies with past QAP policies
and opportunities for changes to future QAP policies.
As a result of this process, the QAP has a new Tribal Housing definition that is directly responsive to the feedback received from Michigan Tribes during
their Consultations. This new definition of Tribal Housing requires that a Tribe or tribally owned entity must be a general partner or managing member of
the LIHTC applicant/owner and must have at least 75% ownership. The new definition further requires that the development must be on lands held by
the Tribe, the tribally designated housing entity, or tribally owned entity in fee or restricted fee; or lands held in trust by the United States of America for
the benefit of the Tribe.
MSHDA staff believe this new Tribal Housing definition will focus resources on those Tribal Housing projects that best align with the needs that were
identified by the Tribes in the Consultations, while also continuing to allow tribally owned economic development entities to participate in projects and
be competitively awarded based on score.

   Obstacles Encountered

Housing availability remains the primary obstacle within homeless crisis response systems across the state. This is compounded by rental costs that continue to rise and
limitations within Fair Market Rent (FMR) calculations. Additionally, many COVID-era programs have ended without other funding alternatives identified or available.
Subrecipient agencies also struggle with frequent staff turnover related to pay and the demands of the job, especially in direct services spaces like emergency shelters and
other crisis intervention services. The same obstacles exist in the HCV program (availability of affordable housing, increased rental costs, as well as incomes not staying on
pace with inflation). Likewise, MSHDA's HCV program was in a shortfall status for the majority of CY24, whereby limited new vouchers were placed in circulation to assist new
families. This compounded the impact on the homeless response system as the resource was not available to move families from shelter or other temporary housing programs
into permanent housing with the voucher program.

   Displacement Impact

   MSHDA's RAHS programs aim to prevent displacement of lower income persons directly by keeping households in their homes and indirectly by influencing broader housing
   market dynamics and neighborhood stability. Rental assistance can stabilize neighborhoods at risk of gentrification by slowing tenant turnover and reducing investor
   incentives to clear buildings of low-income tenants. It can support small and local landlords who may lack financial reserves to maintain their buildings if faced with tenant
   nonpayment. Though households that lose access to rental assistance from MSHDA may lose their home, households receiving rental assistance and grantees receiving
   grants do not directly or indirectly cause displacement.

Obstacles Encountered
The demand for housing and home repairs far exceeds the resources available. Utilization of multiple funding sources and program policies and
procedures is very challenging. Development of a unified program application was difficult to design and develop with a new grants management system
but after launch has become a much more streamlined process for both customers and internal staff.

Economic and Social Benefits to Neighborhoods & Municipalities
All NDD programs are designed to facilitate health and safety repairs to existing units or to encourage the development of new affordable housing. The
programs provide stability to both residents and the housing stock itself.

Displacement Impact
The NDD programs do not allow displacement and/or relocation of existing residents to occur. Instead, the programs are designed to minimize
displacement by providing grant assistance to address health and safety concerns and/or issues within properties to enable "aging in place" through
energy efficiency and/or accessibility improvements.

Obstacles Encountered

The demand for housing and home repairs far exceeds the resources available. Utilization of multiple funding sources and program
policies and procedures is very challenging.

 Economic and Social Benefits to Neighborhoods & Municipalities
 All Housing Solutions programs are designed to facilitate health and safety repairs to existing units or to encourage the development of
 new affordable housing. The programs provide stability for both residents and the housing stock itself.

 Displacement Impact
 No Housing Solutions activities directly resulted in any displacement of low- or moderate-income Michiganders.
